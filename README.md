## What is already known on this topic

- Smartphone-based digital phenotyping can capture real-world behavioural and contextual signals relevant to mental health, including mobility, communication, screen interaction, app use, and activity patterns.
- AI/ML models have increasingly been applied to these data for the assessment and prediction of mental health conditions, but their clinical validity, generalisability, and added value over conventional assessment remain uncertain.

## What this study adds

- This review synthesised **137 studies**, assessing how classical machine learning, deep learning, explainable/interpretable AI, and multimodal approaches have been used in smartphone-based digital phenotyping for mental health.
- The evidence base remains dominated by supervised classification using summary smartphone features, with limited external validation, interpretability, longitudinal forecasting, treatment-response modelling, and multimodal integration.

## How this study might affect research, practice, or policy

- Future research should prioritise larger, more diverse longitudinal cohorts, transparent reporting, leakage-aware validation, calibration, fairness assessment, and clinically meaningful benchmarks.
- Clinical and policy translation should proceed cautiously until digital phenotyping models demonstrate reproducibility, external validity, interpretability, privacy-preserving data governance, and clear incremental clinical utility.

## Abstract

### Background

Smartphones can capture real-world behavioural signals relevant to mental health. The methodological maturity of using Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (AI/ML) models in this field, however, remains uncertain.

### Objective

To evaluate how smartphone-derived digital phenotypes have been used to develop, validate, and report AI/ML models for mental health assessment and prediction, with emphasis on classical machine learning, deep learning, explainable/interpretable AI, and multimodal integration.

### Study selection and analysis

Based on a pre-registered protocol ([OSF: https://osf.io/xv2wb/](https://osf.io/xv2wb/)), we searched seven bibliographic databases from inception to 5 August 2026. Records were screened independently in two stages. Eligible studies used smartphone-derived digital phenotypes to model or predict mental health outcomes in clinical or non-clinical populations. We narratively synthesised study characteristics, sensing streams, AI/ML model development practices, interpretability, and multimodal integration.

### Findings

From **14,266 records**, **137 eligible studies** were included. They mainly focused on depression, anxiety, stress, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder, with fewer studies addressing ADHD, suicide risk, and other clinical conditions/issues.

Smartphone-derived phenotypes were primarily based on location, communication metadata, screen interaction, app use, and activity-related features. Most studies framed AI/ML modelling as supervised prediction or classification, typically using summary features and conventional ML approaches, and relied primarily on internal validation.

Deep learning, external validation, interpretability, fairness assessment, longitudinal forecasting, symptom progression, prognostic forecasting, treatment-response modelling, and multimodal integration were comparatively limited.

### Conclusions and clinical implications

Smartphone-based digital phenotyping is expanding but remains dominated by small, heterogeneous, and internally validated studies using summary features and supervised models. Future work should prioritise larger and more diverse longitudinal cohorts, transparent reporting, leakage-aware validation, interpretability, fairness assessment, and evaluation of clinical utility.
